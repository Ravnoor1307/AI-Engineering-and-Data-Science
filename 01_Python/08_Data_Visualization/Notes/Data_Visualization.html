Welcome to Data Visualization

Everything you have learned until now — lists, loops, NumPy arrays and Pandas DataFrames — has been about storing and manipulating data. In this session we finally give that data a voice. Plotting turns thousands of numbers into a single picture your brain can read in a second, which is why data visualization is the secret weapon of every data analyst: it helps you spot trends, outliers, clusters and proportions long before any formal statistics does.

Matplotlib is the oldest and most widely used plotting library in Python. Its pyplot module, imported by convention as plt, gives you a simple stateful interface: draw a line with plt.plot(x, y), points with plt.scatter(x, y), bars with plt.bar(...), a histogram with plt.hist(...) and a pie with plt.pie(...). Every chart is built on the same idea — a figure (the canvas) holding one or more axes (the actual plot area).

Before writing any plotting code you should ask two questions. First, what kind of data do you have? Data is either numerical (continuous or discrete numbers such as runs scored or price) or categorical (labels or groups such as batsman names or countries). Second, how many columns are you studying? Univariate analysis studies a single column (e.g. a histogram of runs), while bivariate analysis studies two columns together (e.g. average vs strike-rate). Choosing the right chart depends on both answers.

Here is the roadmap for this session:

  1. 2D line plots — trends over a continuous axis such as time.
  2. Scatter plots — the relationship between two numerical columns.
  3. Bar charts — aggregates grouped by categories.
  4. Histograms — the frequency distribution of one numerical column.
  5. Pie charts — the proportion of a whole contributed by each part.
  6. Styles, saving and cleanup — making figures look professional.

Throughout the session we plot real cricket data — run-scoring records of players like Virat Kohli, Rohit Sharma and Chris Gayle — so you can connect every concept to a concrete, relatable example. Let us fire up the library and look at our first chart type.

Taking Matplotlib to the Next Level

In the previous session you mastered the basic chart types — line, scatter, bar, histogram and pie. This session goes deeper: you will learn how to encode a third and even a fourth dimension into a single plot, arrange several charts into one grid, annotate individual data points, and finally plot in three dimensions.

We begin with colored scatter plots. A normal scatter has two dimensions, x and y. By passing a numeric column through the c= argument and choosing a colormap with cmap, the color of each point becomes an extra variable — a cheap clustering peek that lets you see species or classes instantly. Hand-in-hand with this is figure size control: plt.figure(figsize=(width, height)) sets the canvas in inches before drawing, which hugely improves readability when a scatter holds thousands of points.

Next we look at annotations — writing labels directly on the chart with plt.text(x, y, label) and drawing arrows to specific points with plt.annotate(...). Combined with reference lines drawn by plt.axhline(y) and plt.axvline(x), these turn a raw scatter into an insight-rich analysis: thresholds, averages and outliers all become visible at a glance.

The second half of the session is about layout and the third dimension. Subplots let you build multi-panel figures — either with plt.subplots(nrows, ncols) returning a whole array of axes at once, or with the manual fig.add_subplot(row, col, index) style. Then come 3D plots: scatter, line, surface and contour, created by passing projection='3d' when making an axis. Using np.meshgrid and a function of two variables you can visualize surfaces like z = x² + y² or z = sin(x) + cos(y).

Finally we touch on heatmaps with plt.imshow() (a matrix rendered as colored cells) and the extremely convenient Pandas .plot(kind=...) interface, which wraps Matplotlib so a single df.plot(kind='pie') gives you a chart in one line. By the end of the session you will be able to produce publication-ready, multi-dimensional and multi-panel figures for any analysis.

Why Seaborn?

Matplotlib gave you pixel-level control — every line, marker and axis tuned by hand. Seaborn builds on top of Matplotlib and adds three things that make daily analysis dramatically easier: a layer of abstraction (complex statistical plots in one line), better aesthetics (beautiful default colors and styles) and more graph types (density, facet grids, heatmaps, cluster maps and more). If Matplotlib is a toolbox of raw parts, Seaborn is the pre-assembled furniture.

Seaborn functions come in two flavours, and knowing the difference is the single most important idea of the session:

  • Axes-level functions (sns.scatterplot, sns.lineplot, sns.histplot, sns.kdeplot, sns.boxplot, sns.heatmap...) draw onto the current Matplotlib axes and return that axes object. They give you fine control and compose naturally with plt. But they cannot create multi-panel figures by themselves.
  • Figure-level functions (sns.relplot, sns.displot, sns.catplot, sns.lmplot, sns.pairplot...) own an entire figure. They build a FacetGrid internally, so they support faceting with col= and row=, and they manage the legend automatically.

Seaborn's plots fall into a small number of families, the session's roadmap:

  1. Relational plots — the statistical relationship between variables (scatterplot, lineplot).
  2. Distribution plots — how values spread out (histplot, kdeplot, rugplot, plus bivariate densities).
  3. Matrix plots — grids of values colored by magnitude (heatmap, clustermap).

The star feature everywhere is semantic mapping: arguments like hue=, style= and size= map columns onto color, markers and point sizes, letting one scatter carry four variables at once. In this session we use the built-in tips dataset (dinner bills and tips), Gapminder life-expectancy data from plotly.express, and the titanic and iris classics. Import seaborn as sns, and let's look at relational plots first.

What this notebook covers

In Session 25 you met Seaborn's relational, distribution and matrix plot families. These all treat the data as points to scatter, densities to trace or grids to color. Part 2 tackles the plots you reach for when one of your variables is categorical (sex, day, smoker) — plus regression plots and the grid objects that let one figure hold many small panels. Everything runs on the built-in tips and iris datasets.

We start with categorical plots, which split into three groups. Categorical scatter — stripplot, swarmplot — puts every data point over a categorical axis; the trick is jitter (random horizontal offsets so overlapping points separate) or a beeswarm algorithm that packs points without overlap. Categorical distribution — boxplot and violinplot — summarize each category with quartiles or with a box-plus-density shape. Categorical estimate — barplot, pointplot, countplot — reduce each group to a statistic (mean, median, count) and draw bars or points with error bars. Because all of these use the same categorical x-axis, Seaborn bundles them behind one figure-level function: sns.catplot(kind='strip' | 'swarm' | 'box' | 'violin' | 'bar' | 'point' | 'count', ...). The golden rule: a categorical plot has a categorical x (or y) and usually a numeric y.

The notebook then moves to regression plots, where the goal is no longer description but modelling: regplot draws a scatter plus the best-fit line and a 95% confidence band; lmplot is the figure-level version with hue and faceting; residplot plots the residuals (distance between points and line) to check whether a straight line was a fair assumption.

Finally come the grid objects — multi-panel machinery. FacetGrid is a manual grid of panels arranged by col, row and hue onto which you map a plotting function. PairGrid creates a matrix of every numeric-variable pair, letting you choose separate plots for the diagonal (map_diag), upper (map_upper) and lower (map_lower) triangles — pairplot is its one-line default. JointGrid combines a 2D plot with univariate marginals on the edges — jointplot is its default. The notebook closes with sns.get_dataset_names() and sns.load_dataset(), your access to Seaborn's built-in datasets.

Save figure

Once a figure looks right, you can export it: plt.savefig('sample.png') writes the current figure to a file on disk. A few practical rules for saving:

  • dpi controls resolution — dpi=300 gives crisp, print-quality output.
  • bbox_inches='tight' trims the empty padding around the plot.
  • Supported formats include PNG, PDF, SVG and JPG.
  • Call savefig() before plt.show() — once show() runs, the figure is cleared.

3D Surface Plots

A surface plot is the natural 3D extension of a line plot: instead of a curve, we draw a warped sheet — the height of every (x, y) point given by a function z = f(x, y). The recipe is always the same: create 1D x and y vectors with np.linspace, then turn them into 2D grids with np.meshgrid, apply the formula element-wise to build z, and finally call ax.plot_surface(xx, yy, z, cmap='viridis') on a 3D axis. meshgrid produces every combination of x and y coordinates, so z becomes a full grid and the surface is smooth. Matplotlib lets us experiment freely with formulas: z = xx**2 + yy**2 is a paraboloid, z = np.sin(xx) + np.cos(yy) wavelike, and fig.colorbar(p) adds a legend for the height.

Contour Plots

A contour plot takes the same z = f(x, y) grid used for a 3D surface and projects it down onto the 2D plane, drawing level curves — lines of constant height, like the rings on a topographic map. ax.contour(xx, yy, z, cmap='viridis') draws thin lines for each level, while ax.contourf(xx, yy, z, cmap='viridis') fills the areas between levels with color, making the shape easier to read. Each color band represents a range of z values, and fig.colorbar(p) maps colors back to numbers. Contours reveal the layout of a function instantly: z = sin(xx) + cos(yy) appears as a pattern of alternating ridges (peaks) and valleys (troughs). They are indispensable for understanding functions of two variables before you ever fit a model to them.

  1. Relational Plots

Relational plots answer "how do two variables move together?". They are the heart of bivariate analysis. When one of the variables is categorical or you simply want to see every observation, use a scatterplot; when the x-axis is continuous (like a year) and you want the trend, use a lineplot.   • hue='sex' — color points by a category.
  • style='time' — change marker shape per category.
  • size='size' — scale point size by a numeric column.

Because the figure-level relplot returns a FacetGrid, it also accepts col and row to split the data into a grid of panels automatically — something the axes-level functions cannot do. Comparison across countries, across time, across groups — relational plots are your first stop for almost any pairing of numeric columns.

  1. Distribution Plots

Distribution plots are built for univariate analysis — studying one column at a time. Their questions: What is the range of values? What is the central tendency? Is the data bimodal (two peaks)? Are there outliers? Three tools cover most needs:

  • sns.histplot — the familiar histogram: data is binned into intervals and each bin is drawn as a bar whose height is the count (the frequency).

The figure-level sns.displot(kind='hist' | 'kde' | 'ecdf') wraps all of them and adds hue, fill, and faceting. A histogram can even be applied to a categorical variable as a count of rows per category. This section is about shape: the distribution tells you whether data clusters around a mean, stretches right, or splits into peaks.

Regression Plots

  • regplot
  • lmplot

In the simplest invocation, both functions draw a scatterplot of two variables, x and y, and then fit the regression model y ~ x and plot the resulting regression line and a 95% confidence interval for that regression.

Regression plots — regplot, lmplot, residplot

A natural step after describing data is to model it. sns.regplot(data, x='total_bill', y='tip') draws the scatter and then fits a simple linear regression tip ~ total_bill, plotting the fitted line plus a shaded 95% confidence band for the mean. regplot is an axes-level function — it draws on the current axes and therefore has no hue parameter. sns.lmplot(...) is its figure-level sibling: it builds a FacetGrid internally, so it accepts hue, col and row, and is the right choice when you want separate regression lines per group (here, per sex). sns.residplot(data, x='total_bill', y='tip') plots the residuals on the y-axis — every point's vertical distance from the regression line. If the residuals form a random cloud around zero, a straight line is a good fit; if they curve or fan out, linearity or constant variance is violated. These three plots are your first look at inference: not just what the data looks like, but how strongly and how well two variables relate.

So far every catplot(kind=...) produced a single panel. The moment you add col='smoker' and row='time', the same call multiplies itself into a grid of panels — one for every combination of the two faceting columns (here 2 × 2 = 4 panels). Faceting is more powerful than a hue mapping: rather than splitting colors inside one crowded axes, each category gets its own clean panel, so the shapes of all the boxplots remain legible. You can combine kind, hue, col and row freely — e.g. a violin per day (cols) for each time (rows), colored by sex. Facets answer the truly multi-variate question: does the pattern hold for both smokers and non-smokers, at lunch and at dinner? Whenever a figure-level function feels cramped, reach for col= and row= before you give up on readability.

Plotly Express — Interactive Data Visualization

The Plotly Roadmap

Here is the mental map you should keep for the whole session — the three layers of the Plotly stack:

  1. Plotly Go (plotly.graph_objects as go) — the foundational, low-level API. You think in traces: go.Scatter(x=..., y=..., mode='markers') creates one trace, several traces make the data, a go.Layout(title=..., xaxis=..., yaxis=...) drives the look, and go.Figure(data, layout) assembles the plot. Full pixel-level control, explicit and verbose.
  2. Plotly Express (plotly.express as px) — the high-level, one-line API built on top of Go. px.scatter(df, x=..., y=..., color=..., size=..., hover_name=...) handles traces, layout and legend automatically and returns a ready figure. Prefer px everywhere unless you need Go's low-level machinery (surfaces, contours and subplot grids are Go's turf).
  3. Dash — the application layer. Dash takes Express/Go figures and wires them into interactive multi-page web apps with dropdowns, sliders and live updates. Dash is out of scope for this notebook but it is the reason Plotly figures embed so naturally into browsers.

Rule of thumb for this session: start in Express (px), drop to Go when the chart type is not available in Express (surface, contour, raw circuit layout), and remember Dash exists for anything that must react to user input.
